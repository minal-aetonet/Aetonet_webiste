Most cloud migrations are sold as a lift-and-shift exercise: move the servers, keep the architecture, collect the savings. In practice, teams that migrate this way often end up paying more than they did on-premises, because the cost model of the cloud punishes idle capacity and rewards workloads that are actually designed for it.

Match the migration pattern to the workload

Not every system deserves the same treatment. A batch job that runs nightly is a poor candidate for a rehost; it’s a strong candidate for a fully managed, pay-per-use service. A latency-sensitive transactional system may need a careful refactor, not a rehost at all.

Lift-and-shift buys you a data center address change. It rarely buys you the economics people expect from “the cloud.”

A phased approach that limits risk

  1. Inventory and classify. Group systems by criticality, data sensitivity, and how well they tolerate re-architecture.
  2. Migrate the low-risk tier first. Internal tools and stateless services validate your pipeline before anything customer-facing moves.
  3. Right-size before automating. Automating a wasteful configuration just makes the waste run faster.
  4. Set a cost ceiling per workload. Track spend per system from day one, not after the first surprising invoice.

Important risks to manage

  • Data egress costs that were never modeled during planning
  • Compliance and residency requirements that differ by region
  • Team skill gaps that turn “managed services” into unmanaged ones
  • Vendor lock-in from proprietary managed offerings

The organizations that come out ahead treat migration as a sequence of small, reversible decisions rather than one large bet—each phase funded by the savings or capability gains of the last.
